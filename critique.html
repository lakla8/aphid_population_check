Model audit · wheat aphid population dynamics

Aphid Catastrophe Audit

A full reproduction of a published catastrophe-theory model of wheat aphids, the eight things that go wrong with it, and what to build instead.

Li Y., Hu Z., Li Z., Kong Y., Piyaratne M.K.D.K., Wang B., Zhao H. (2020). Generalized population dynamics model of aphids in wheat based on catastrophe theory. BioSystems 198:104217.
Field data: Shaanxi winter wheat, 1997–2002, 62 survey dates across six seasons.
SUMMARY

What holds and what doesn’t

Every number in the paper reproduces. The arithmetic is correct and the catastrophe algebra is exact. The problems are all downstream of that: a term the fit switches off, a classification that reduces to one inequality, and a control variable that turns out to be a proxy for the state variable.

§ 2

The density-dependence term is switched off

The model’s central innovation is the correction term 1 − eNθ/K, which is where the meteorological index enters. Decomposing the rate across the 55 fitted points shows it contributes nothing measurable.

brake  −r·m·e·N²/K               median 1.3e-10      0.00%

full model                    R² = 0.6375

K forced to 10⁴                R² = −3.2201

Swinging e from −2 to +2 changes the rate by 2.7×10−9 aphids/day, against a median |dN/dt| of 55.6.

So the entire meteorological apparatus — the catastrophe-progression method, the Spearman screening of Table A1, the butterfly membership functions, all of Section 2.4.1 — feeds a term that is numerically inert. e affects the region labels, through ξ = K/e in the control variables, while having no effect on the dynamics.

Why it switched itself off

Two mechanisms in the same equation live on disjoint density scales:

predation matters around   N ~ d     ~ 7×10²
brake matters around       N ~ K/e   ~ 6×10¹¹
largest density observed             7.5×10³

Nothing observed approaches K/e, so the likelihood is flat in K and the estimator sends it wherever makes the term vanish. The diagnosis is printed in the paper itself:

The authors’ own starting guess for K was 9 649 — a sensible ceiling, just above the observed maximum of 7 480. The optimiser moved it by a factor of 2 852 and returned a standard error of 4×10−10, a relative precision of 10−17, below machine epsilon. That is not a precise estimate; it is a singular Jacobian. Every other parameter has a sane 12–16% error.

A purely internal check needs no fitting at all: K and d are both densities in the same equation. d = 697 aphids per 100 stems is a reasonable half-saturation. K = 2.75×107 is 3 700× the largest count ever recorded.

§ 3

The model cannot be integrated

The paper estimates Eq. (5) from instantaneous rates and never simulates it. Integrated, four of six seasons diverge. Three things compound:

  1. The brake is inert. At N = 500, the correction is 1.000018 for e = −1 and 0.999982 for e = +1.
  2. The predator saturates at P·k, so growth rN overtakes it permanently once N > Pk/r — only 1 200 aphids at P = 5 PU.
  3. What remains is r ≈ 0.39/day, a doubling every 1.8 days.

The sign of e decides only how the divergence ends, not whether it happens — a season runs away at e = 0 too. For e > 0 there is an equilibrium at K/e ≈ 107, finite but not a field density; for e < 0 there is no positive equilibrium at all and growth turns quadratic, reaching infinity in finite time.

Not repairable by refitting 
e = −1, P = 4          positive equilibria
paper's parameters     none — diverges
K = 10⁴ (5000× smaller) none — still diverges
k = 10⁵                refuge at 0.48 aphids
d = 10                 refuge at 0.10 aphids

Shrinking K makes it worse: when e < 0 the term is 1 + |e|N/K, so a smaller K strengthens the accelerator. Only the predation parameters can create a refuge, at values that are not credible — k = 105 means one predator unit eating 100 000 aphids a day.

This is not a bad fit of r, K, k, d. It is the functional form.

§ 4

The catastrophe apparatus does no work

The control variables reach |u| ~ 1012 and |v| ~ 1018, but almost all of that magnitude is K/e scaling that cancels out of the discriminant. The classification collapses to a single inequality on predator density:

P* = 2 d r m / k = 5.81 PU / 100 stems 2.90 on a spray day — and independent of e to four decimals

Classifying all 62 dates by “P > P*, else sign of e” reproduces the published regions 62/62.

Climbing to the swallowtail adds a third control variable that is not independent:

u = d² − ξ    w = −d²ξ    ⇒    w = d²u − d⁴ exact to 3×10−16 over all 62 dates · rank of [u, v, w] = 2 of 3

Both normal forms are built from the same two numbers, ξ = K/e and η = kP/rm. The cusp spends two coordinates on them; the swallowtail wastes a third. The swallowtail classification is a perfect 1:1 relabelling of the cusp regions — 27 / 17 / 18, identical growth rates, identical p = 0.106.

§ 5

The classification has no predictive power

Regions are meant to mean I = safe, III = outbreak. Taking the region at the start of each of the 56 between-survey intervals and measuring the growth that followed:

Region at startnmean growth / dayfraction that grew
I — “safe”26+0.1810.88

Region I grew faster than region III; the contrast is p = 0.56. Five of the six largest population jumps in the dataset started in region I. The paper’s headline 1997 transition, NO5 (335) → NO6 (591), is ×1.76 over six days — 9.9%/day, ordinary aphid growth. The label changed because e crossed zero, not because the population did anything abrupt.

§ 6

The one working variable is endogenous

This is the deepest problem, and it invalidates the framework rather than the fit. The whole model rests on P as an exogenous control — a lever you pull to manage aphids. It is nothing of the kind.

The apparent predator effect is entirely confounded with starting density. And region II, defined by P > 5.81, selects populations 28× larger: median N of 1 610 against region I’s 57. The “dynamic control threshold” is a threshold on aphid density wearing a predator costume.

This is also why every model version fails on the same two seasons. In 2002 the population needed to grow at 0.217/day; the fitted growth rate there is around 0.36/day, so it could. But P was 20–39 PU, so the model parked the population at its refuge equilibrium. It read high predator counts as strong control when they actually meant lots of aphids had attracted lots of predators. The causality is backwards.

This does not show predators don’t matter. It shows their effect cannot be identified from data in which P and N are collinear at ρ = 0.84. That is an identification problem, and no functional form solves it.

§ 7

Bookkeeping

Three smaller items, none of which affects the paper’s published results, but each of which would mislead anyone building on it.

The discriminant is numerically hazardous

Computing Δ = 27v² + 4u³ directly loses up to 12.1 decimal digits to cancellation, leaving about 3.8 of double precision’s 15.95. Measured against an 80-digit evaluation: the naive form carries a median relative error of 8×10−8 (worst 8×10−5); the factored Eq. (14) carries 2×10−16. On this dataset the sign — and so the region — survives either way, but with under four significant digits of margin that is luck, not method.

Table 2 is derived from a different equation

Its θ ≥ 2 rows carry Kθ where Eq. (5) as printed gives K. Verified symbolically: the table matches a model with 1 − e(N/K)θ, not the paper’s 1 − eNθ/K. The two coincide at θ = 1 — the row actually used — so the published results are unaffected.

A misnumbered citation

Section 3.3.1 reads “In 1999, sudden jumps occurred from NO 26 (5) to NO 27 (12)”. In the paper’s own Table 3, NO26 = 146 and NO27 = 56; the values 5 and 12 belong to NO18 and NO19. The surrounding narrative — early April, jointing stage, no aphids yet — confirms NO18→19 was meant.

§ 8

None of this is new

The genre critique is forty-nine years old and was published in Nature.

Zahler R.S. & Sussmann H.J. (1977). Claims and accomplishments of applied catastrophe theory. Nature 269:759–763. — long version: Sussmann & Zahler, Synthese 37:117–216 (1978).

Reviewing Zeeman’s applications to nerve impulse, prison riots, stock-market crashes and war, they made four charges. Each maps onto a measurement above:

Zahler & Sussmann, 1977Measured here
The catastrophes “only restate the fact that discontinuities exist”Regions reduce to P > 5.81; the “catastrophes” are 9.9%/day growth
“No deep mathematical results are actually used”The swallowtail’s third control is w = d²u − d⁴
Thom’s theorem is “inherently uninformative for applications”All seven exponent pairs give the identical partition
Hypotheses “ambiguous or far-fetched”K = 2.75×107, 3 700× the largest observation

Their conclusion was that catastrophe theory had “made no significant contributions to biology and the social sciences, and [had] no advantage over other better-established mathematical tools”. There were rebuttals, and the settled modern view is narrower: the mathematics is rigorous and the taxonomy is useful; the empirical programme was oversold.

Why this particular paper went unchecked is mundane. It has roughly two citations. It looks fine from outside — R2 = 0.6473, p < 10−11, six years of field data. Catching the problem requires decomposing the terms, profiling the likelihood, or trying to integrate the equation, none of which is standard review practice. And the lineage was self-reinforcing: Zhao 1988 → Zhao 2005 → Wei 2009 → Li 2012 → Piyaratne 2013 → Wu 2014 → this paper, one group, each step adding a term to the same equation, the form inherited rather than re-derived. The tell sat in Table 4 through review and publication.

PART 2

How to build this better

The phenomenon is real. Insect-outbreak bistability — a refuge state, a threshold, an outbreak state, with hysteresis between them — is well established and has lived since 1978 in Ludwig, Jones & Holling’s spruce budworm model. What has to go is the apparatus, not the ecology. These are ordered: step 1 alone would have caught almost everything above.

  1. Non-dimensionalise before fitting anything

    Scale by the model’s own characteristic quantities — n = N/d, τ = r m t — and Eq. (5) becomes

    dn/dτ = n(1 − n/κ) − ρ n²/(n²+1) κ = K/(e d)   ρ = Pk/(r m d)

    Four parameters collapse to two groups, and it is immediately visible that κ is 1011 while the data live near n ~ 1. No numerics required. Every quantity in the model is a characteristic density; write them all out and compare them to the data range before estimating anything.

  2. Report the bifurcation you actually have

    With the dead term removed, the interior equilibria solve a quadratic:

    n± = [ρ ± √(ρ² − 4)] / 2    real iff ρ ≥ 2 verified against numerical roots to four significant figures

    That is a fold — a saddle-node in one control parameter, the simplest object in the hierarchy — and ρ = 2 is exactly the P* threshold. A closed form with an interpretable threshold beats any normal-form diagram. The paper excluded the fold in its opening move, on the grounds that “stable behavior cannot be observed” in it, which is backwards for its own data.

  3. Make the predator a state variable, not a dial

    The single most important change. P is collinear with N at ρ = 0.84, so a one-state-variable model treating it as exogenous has the causality backwards. Two routes, both standard:

    • Two-state predator–prey, with a numerical response giving P its own dynamics. The paper’s own closing paragraph proposes exactly this and then publishes the one-state version.
    • Predator-exclusion experiments — cages, manipulated P. This is the only route that identifies the predation effect rather than assuming it; observational data of this structure never can.

  4. Put weather where it can act, in a form that can go negative

    Weather currently enters only through the inert term. Move it onto the growth rate, linearly:

    r(e) = r0 + a e

    Linear matters. e is standardised on [−3.6, +1.9], so this is its first-order expansion — and unlike an exponential it can turn negative, letting the population shrink unaided in cold, wet weather. That mortality is real and has to live somewhere; the paper itself invokes rainfall “washing” aphids off the crop in 2002.

  5. Fix what the data cannot see; bound what biology constrains

    A parameter with no support in the data should be set, not estimated. Fixing the ceiling at K = 104 moves pooled fit quality only from 0.54 to 0.56 across an order of magnitude of K — it genuinely doesn’t matter, which is precisely why fitting it is dangerous.

    Bounding matters just as much. A trial version here put weather on predation as k(e) = k0ece. It fitted better — pooled log-R2 0.645 against 0.563 — and was biological nonsense: the optimiser bought low-e mortality by inflating predation to a median of 540 and a maximum of 45 000 aphids per predator unit per day, against a literature range of 50–150 for an adult Coccinella septempunctata. An unbounded exponential on a rate with a hard biological ceiling will always be abused that way. The worse-fitting, bounded model is the one to report.

  6. Add inputs, not coordinates

    Climbing fold → cusp → swallowtail adds axes to a description of the same seven numbers. What the model is missing is information:

    • Immigration. Winter wheat is colonised each spring by alates and Eq. (5) has no immigration term at all. The Rothamsted Insect Survey’s 12.2 m suction-trap network — 16 traps, daily April–November, 1964 to present — is a measured forcing for exactly this.
    • Crop growth stage. Aphid carrying capacity on wheat tracks phenology, and the season ends when the crop senesces. The paper discusses this and never models it.
    • Temperature-dependent predation. The paper cites Ding et al. (1983) and Zamani et al. (2006) for precisely this and uses neither.

  7. Fit trajectories, diagnose identifiability, hold out years

    Fitting instantaneous rates against spline derivatives hides the divergence entirely — R2 = 0.64 on derivatives from a model that reaches infinity in finite time. Integrate and compare to the observations. Then profile every parameter individually; a flat profile is the signal to fix that parameter rather than report it. And hold out whole seasons: all three model versions tested here fit 1997, 1998 and 2000 and fail on 2001 and 2002, which is only visible with a held-out year.

  8. Keep the bistability, drop the machinery

    You do not need Thom’s classification to find a saddle-node; you need to solve a quadratic. Catastrophe theory’s legitimate role is as a taxonomy of which bifurcations are structurally stable — a conceptual guide for knowing what to look for, not a fitting method. The phenomenon it was reached for here is alive and well under other names: alternative stable states, critical transitions, regime shifts.

A model worth trying

Putting the above together, with S for crop growth stage and I(t) for measured immigration:

dN/dt = r(e, S) N (1 − N/K(S)) + I(t) − f(N, P)
dP/dt = ε f(N, P) − μP P f is the Holling response · insecticide is an impulse N → mN, and kills P too

Two state variables, immigration as an external forcing, phenology entering both the rate and the ceiling, and predators with their own dynamics so their density is an outcome rather than an assumption. Non-dimensionalise it before fitting, fix what the data cannot resolve, and hold out seasons.

What the data would need to support it

The binding constraint is not modelling technique, it is measurement. Paired aphid and natural-enemy counts at the same visit, weekly, over several seasons — which is exactly what makes the Shaanxi dataset unusual, and exactly what most published aphid datasets lack. Add to it: suction-trap counts for immigration, recorded crop growth stage, and at minimum one season of predator-exclusion plots to break the P–N collinearity. Weather is the easy part; any reanalysis product supplies daily temperature, humidity, rainfall and sunshine.

All three model versions tested here fail on the same two seasons. That is not three functional forms each wrong in its own way — it is one missing covariate. No re-specification of the right-hand side fixes that.
